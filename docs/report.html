Narrate

A native macOS app that turns a DRM-free EPUB into a chaptered M4B audiobook, rendered entirely on a 16 GB M2 in one night, and checking its own work before you ever press play.

29 October 2026 · Response to the proposed pipeline “EPUB → ebooklib → normalize → chunk → Qwen3-TTS → ffmpeg → M4B”

Recommendation
Build v1 on Kokoro-82M running on MLX, not on Qwen3-TTS. Spend the hours that saves on listening back to every sentence automatically.
  • Measured on this M2: Kokoro renders an 11-hour novel in about 2.2 hours. Qwen3-TTS 0.6B would take about 19 hours, filled all 16 GB of memory, and got 30% of the words wrong. Qwen3-TTS is out on this machine.
  • For fiction, the biggest quality lever isn't the model but getting the names right. A per-book pronunciation list, confirmed before rendering, fixes hundreds of sentences at once.
  • No existing tool (abogen, audiblez, ebook2audiobook) checks the audio it produced or can re-render a single sentence. That gap is the reason to build Narrate.
  • Keep the engine behind a small plugin interface, so Qwen3-TTS can be an opt-in for people with bigger machines.

Your ranking is right for an NVIDIA box and wrong for this Mac, and the bake-off below shows that with measurements rather than estimates. Qwen3-TTS is the better narrator on paper, and its Apache-2.0 license is fine. On a base M2, though, even the 0.6B model runs slower than real time and garbles the text. Kokoro finishes a novel in about two hours, which leaves most of the night for the listen-back check and retries.

Two findings from the research change the design itself.

  • Kokoro silently drops words it doesn't know. It falls back to espeak-ng for words missing from its pronunciation dictionary, and if espeak isn't installed, those words are skipped without an error. espeak-ng is also GPL-3.0, which is awkward to bundle. The pronunciation step below covers exactly those words, so espeak can be an optional download, and the listen-back check catches any word that still goes missing.
  • Use Parakeet, not Whisper, for the check. Parakeet TDT on MLX is English-only and returns word timings. On this M2 it ran at 0.06–0.22× real time, so checking an 11-hour book takes 40 minutes to 2 hours. The variation was probably thermal. Unlike Whisper, it doesn't hallucinate fluent text into silence, which matters when the whole point is catching silence.

Prior art: audiblez and abogen already turn EPUBs into M4Bs with Kokoro, and abogen has a GUI, queues and subtitles. Neither checks its output, and both re-render only whole chapters. Read abogen's EPUB handling before writing the ingest stage; there's no need to rediscover its edge cases.

Your pipeline, revised

The shape you proposed is right: chapters, chunks, per-chunk files, then ffmpeg to M4B. Most of my changes make each chunk addressable, checkable and cheap to redo.

Keep
Chunk, render, concatenate. Never feed whole chapters.

The chunk is the unit you retry, cache, check and fix. With Kokoro, though, chunks have to be smaller than your 300–1000 characters. Kokoro accepts at most 510 phoneme tokens per call (about 30 seconds of speech) and truncates anything longer. That means one to three sentences, roughly 150–350 characters, split only at sentence boundaries and never across paragraphs.

Change
Split chapters by the table of contents, not by spine file.

An EPUB's spine files and its chapters often don't line up. One file can hold three chapters, or one chapter can span two files. Walk the nav/NCX table of contents and its #anchor targets instead, and fall back to detecting <h1>/<h2> headings when the TOC is broken.

Change
Read the EPUB's semantic tags before guessing.

EPUB 3 marks sections with epub:type values such as copyright-page, toc, index, footnote and noteref. Use them to drop boilerplate and to read each footnote right after the sentence that cites it. Heuristics, and the optional local LLM, step in only when the tags are missing, which is common in older books.

Change
Insert silence; don't crossfade.

Crossfading speech smears consonants and shifts timing. Instead, trim each chunk's own leading and trailing silence and insert exact pauses based on the text's structure. Roughly 0.25 s between sentences, 0.6 s between paragraphs, 1.4 s at a scene break (* * *), and 2 s after a chapter title. The gaps then sound like a narrator breathing, not like seams.

Change
Name chunk files by their content, not by number.

037.wav breaks as soon as you edit chapter 7 and every later chunk shifts by one. Instead, name each file by a hash of the normalized text, voice, speed and engine version. An edit then re-renders only the chunk that changed, and an engine upgrade automatically invalidates the old audio.

Add
Pronunciation pre-flight.

Before rendering, list every word that isn't in the speech model's pronunciation dictionary (Livesey, Trelawney, Hispaniola), plus words with well-known traps (boatswain, forecastle, gunwale), ranked by how often they occur. You confirm a “sounds like” spelling once, and it applies throughout the book.

Add
Listen back to every chunk.

Parakeet transcribes each rendered chunk. The app compares the transcript with the source text and checks speaking rate, silences and clipping. Only the outliers are flagged for you. This is what makes “review flags only” trustworthy.

Add
Encode once, after a loudness pass.

Concatenate all the audio uncompressed first, then run two-pass loudnorm to −18 LUFS with a true peak of −1.5 dB. Encode to AAC only once, with the macOS aac_at encoder at 64 kb/s mono. Encoding once also avoids AAC's start-up silence at every join.

Change
The LLM never rewrites prose.

A small local model may classify sections (front matter or story?) and suggest pronunciations, and you approve each suggestion. It never paraphrases the text: an LLM that quietly “fixes” a sentence in chapter 19 is a bug you'd never find.

The pipeline

Eight stages. Each one writes a file that the next stage reads, and each can resume after a crash or quit. Outlined stages are new compared with your sketch.

1 · ingest

Unpack the EPUB

Reads metadata, cover, spine and table of contents. Splits chapters at TOC anchors and tags front matter, notes and scene breaks.

book.json
new2 · contents

Pick chapters

Shows the detected structure. Copyright, TOC, index and ads are skipped by default; you can toggle anything.

book.json · include[]
3 · normalize

Make text speakable

Expands numbers, dates, Roman numerals in headings and abbreviations (Dr., Capt.). Handles dialect apostrophes (’em, o’, Cap’n). Turns dashes and ellipses into pauses.

speech.json
new4 · lexicon

Pronunciations

Lists unknown words by frequency. The local LLM can pre-fill “sounds like” spellings, which you confirm. Spellings are converted to phonemes.

lexicon.toml
5 · chunk

Sentence chunks

One to three sentences, under 510 phoneme tokens. Each chunk records the pause that follows it: sentence, paragraph, scene or chapter.

chunks.sqlite
6 · synth

Render audio

Kokoro on MLX, 24 kHz mono. Each chunk is cached by content hash and never rendered twice.

cache/ab/ab31…f2.wav
new7 · check

Listen back

Parakeet transcribes each chunk. Checks word errors against the text, characters per second, silence and clipping. Retries once with a different split; anything still failing is flagged.

qa table · chunks.sqlite
8 · assemble

Chapters → M4B

Trims, inserts gaps, normalizes loudness, then encodes once to AAC. Adds QuickTime chapter markers, cover and tags.

Treasure Island.m4b
The fix loop. Editing a chunk's text or adding a pronunciation in Review re-runs stages 3–7 for only the chunks it touches. Fixing “Livesey” re-renders the 212 chunks that contain the name, about 40 seconds of work, then stage 8 rebuilds the M4B in about a minute.

How it's built

Two processes and one folder per book. The Swift app owns the interface, playback and files. A bundled Python worker owns everything that touches a model. They talk JSON-RPC over stdin/stdout, which means the worker doubles as a command-line tool at no extra cost.

The engine contract

Each engine implements four things: voices(), max_chunk(), synthesize(text, voice, speed) → pcm, and an engine_id that becomes part of every cache key. Kokoro is the default. A Qwen3-TTS plugin only has to satisfy the same contract, and the chunker reads max_chunk() to size chunks for it. Your original ranking still applies; it just moves to people with an NVIDIA GPU or 32 GB or more of memory. On this Mac, the bake-off ruled it out.

The local LLM, on a leash

It runs once before rendering and is then unloaded, so it never competes with the speech models for 16 GB of memory. A 3–4B instruct model, 4-bit quantized and run through mlx-lm, needs about 2.5 GB. It has two jobs, and both produce suggestions you approve. It classifies unlabelled sections (front matter or story?) and proposes sounds-like spellings for unknown words. Skip it and the app still works; you type the spellings yourself.

Risks

One voice for eight hoursDecided: a female narrator, so the default is af_heart, the best-rated Kokoro voice, with af_bella as the alternative. Kokoro's weaker male voices no longer matter. What's left is listener fatigue: confirm in M0 that af_heart at about 0.94× speed is still pleasant after an hour, not just after a minute.
Prosody across ~3,800 separate chunksEach chunk is spoken without hearing the one before it, so intonation can reset every sentence or two. To soften this, fill chunks up to the token limit within a paragraph, and keep a quote together with its tag (“…,” said he). Judge it by ear in M0, not on paper.
False alarms from the listen-back checkDialect and archaic spellings (’em, fo’c’sle, ye) won't match the transcript even when the audio is right. Normalize both sides before comparing, ignore punctuation, and remember accepted flags per word. Tune thresholds on one real book until under 0.5% of chunks get flagged.
Shipping Python inside a Mac appEvery .so file in the bundle has to be signed, the app needs notarizing, and it's about 400 MB before models. Ship the worker as a signed helper, download models on first run, and keep the CLI installable separately with uv tool install.
Licenses in the bundleespeak-ng is GPL-3.0, Parakeet's weights are CC-BY-4.0, and Supertonic's are OpenRAIL-M. Bundle none of them; download each on first use with its license shown. Use an LGPL ffmpeg build, without --enable-gpl.
The variety of EPUBs out thereBroken tables of contents, chapter titles as images, footnotes as bare superscript links. Keep 20 real public-domain EPUBs from Standard Ebooks and Project Gutenberg as ingest test fixtures from day one.
Apple Books quirksWhether Apple Books needs the audiobook media-type tag, and how it handles faststart, couldn't be confirmed. Import a test M4B into Apple Books and BookPlayer in M1 instead of trusting ffprobe alone.

Settled after the first draft

No questions are open. These four decisions came after the first version of this report.

  • Narrator: female, Kokoro af_heartaf_bella is the alternative. Kokoro's weaker male voices are no longer a factor.
  • Chapter titles: number plus title, spoken“Chapter One. The Old Sea-dog at the Admiral Benbow.” Part titles are spoken the same way (“Part One. The Old Buccaneer.”). Numbers are spelled out from the TOC, not read off the heading text.
  • Longest book in one night: about 150,000 wordsThat's roughly 16.5 hours of audio and 4.5–6.5 hours of work on this M2, by the bake-off measurements. Longer books still work, because a render that runs past morning picks up where it stopped.
